# Validate the soil engine with three scalable cases

No single open dataset exists that pairs a characterised soil, a 20 t excavator and measured digging forces. A credible validation ladder for the Newton implicit MPM simulator (8 cm grid, 4 cm particles) can still be built from three published cases. Each is either dimensionless or can be geometrically scaled until the 8 cm grid resolves it. **First, a scaled 2D granular column collapse** (the aluminium-rod test plus an aspect-ratio sweep) checks the dry-sand Drucker-Prager flow and deposit. **Second, the Makange et al. 2021 soil-bin subsoiler table** (CC BY 4.0, clay, 0.15 to 0.30 m depth, tabulated horizontal and vertical forces) checks cohesive tool forces. **Third, the Aoshima & Servin Komatsu WA320-7 full-scale loading study** checks bulk bucket forces and payload against a published real-time error budget of about 10%. Most classic lab experiments (narrow tines, 2 to 5 cm cuts, 25 cm rover wheels, 36 mm CPT cones) are below the grid's resolution and can only be used through scaling laws or analytical reference curves. The peer simulators confirm that this is normal practice. Chrono CRM validates on mm-scale cratering, cones and a single wheel. AGX validates mostly against high-resolution DEM and one wheel-loader field test. Vortex publishes an analytical blade equation rather than experiments. Newton's implicit MPM has no published physical validation at all. Realistic acceptance bands from the literature are **±10 to 15% on runout and mean tool force, and ±15% on payload**. Shallow cuts of two cells or fewer will underpredict draft, and implicit MPM needs locking mitigation before any of these targets is reachable.

## The 8 cm grid filters out most of the experimental literature

The simulator's resolution sets the selection rule. A failing soil mass needs roughly 10 to 20 cells to form a credible shear band, which means about 1 to 2 m of geometry. A tool needs about 0.3 m of width and a 0.1 to 0.5 m cut to be represented at all. Against that rule, the classic soil-bin literature mostly fails. Narrow tines under 0.1 m wide, Haeri & Skonieczny's MPM-validated blade cuts at **2 and 5 cm depth** in GRC-1 lunar simulant ([arXiv 2111.01523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.01523)), and the scaled Caterpillar D3K2 bulldozer blade are all too small for direct comparison. The D3K2 blade was tested at 1/8 and 1/13 scale with a cut of 20% of blade height, about 27 mm at 1/8 scale ([J. Terramechanics 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489819301466); [J. Terramechanics 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489824000430)). The same filter removes the terramechanics staples. Chrono CRM's MGRU3 wheel has a radius of **0.25 m and width 0.2 m**, run in a 0.25 m deep soil bin ([arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05643)). Its cone-penetration validation used 9 to 20 mm cones in a 0.1 m container. Standard bevameter plates of 5 to 20 cm would span one or two cells.

Two routes recover the usable data. The first is **dimensionless or similitude scaling**. Drucker-Prager with cohesion has no internal length scale and is rate-independent, so a benchmark scaled up by a factor λ behaves identically if cohesion is scaled to hold c/(ρgL) constant. Forces then scale as λ³ and times as √λ. This is a property of the model, so the scaled run tests the discretisation, not the physics. The scaling-law literature supports it for granular collapse: a 2025 study found the critical aspect ratio of **a = 1.7 unchanged across gravity levels** ([Comput. Geotech. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266352X25001569)). Real grains do carry a size effect. Man et al. showed that runout depends on column size relative to grain size, not only on aspect ratio ([GRL 2021](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021GL096054)), so absolute runout coefficients carry perhaps 10 to 20% uncertainty while exponents and transitions are robust. The second route is **analytical reference curves at field scale**. These include the Griffiths & Lane slope, Prandtl bearing capacity, Rankine wall thrust, Bekker-Wong pressure-sinkage with Wong's sand, loam and clay rows, and Wagner's fundamental equation of earthmoving for a flat blade. They are verification rather than validation, but they are exactly resolvable at 8 cm.

## Granular collapse is the cleanest first test, and the rod experiment anchors it

Granular column collapse is the most widely shared MPM, SPH and DEM benchmark. Its runout follows a piecewise law in aspect ratio a = H0/L0: linear for short columns and a power law above a transition near a ≈ 1.7 to 3. Numerical studies report exponents of **0.68 to 0.77 for a > 4** ([arXiv 2206.00915](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.00915)). The quantitative MPM case is the 2D aluminium-rod test attributed to Bui et al. 2008 and re-documented by Nguyen et al. 2017 ([Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/a-new-sph-based-approach-to-simulation-of-granular-flows-using-vi/)). A **0.2 m wide by 0.1 m high** rod column is released and comes to rest at about **0.607 s with final runout about 0.48 m** from the wall. Free surfaces were digitised at 0.109, 0.235 and 0.607 s. The rods barely move out of plane, which makes this a true plane-strain test ([arXiv 2609.26104](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26104)). The case matters directly for Newton. Xie, Navas & López-Querol ran an *implicit* B-spline MPM on it and found that **only the modified F-bar variant matched the experimental runout history**. Their reference used about 40 cells per column height ([arXiv 2609.26104](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26104)). Newton's implicit MPM with about 2 particles per cell per axis runs into the same volumetric-locking problem, so this test checks the solver's numerics as well as the constitutive model.

Granular collapse also covers cohesion. DEM and continuum studies show that cohesion mainly shortens the final deposit and roughens it, with little effect on collapse velocity ([Langlois et al. 2015](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014JF003330); [cohesive slump, continuum and discrete](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344807155_Cohesive_slump_The_Slumping_of_a_Cohesive_Granular_Column_Continuum_and_Discrete_Modelling)). That is the behaviour a Drucker-Prager cohesion term should reproduce. A sweep of c/(ρgH0) therefore extends the same harness to the moist-sand and loam presets. The sweep should find a no-collapse threshold close to the classical vertical-cut critical height. It should also find block-versus-continuous failure regimes like those in the wet-collapse phase diagrams ([arXiv 2402.07285](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.07285)). The Bond-number literature defines Bo in opposite senses, so check each paper's definition before comparing trends. A rate-independent model will match deposits better than front-velocity histories for tall columns, where inertial μ(I) effects dominate. Velocity misfit at a > 3 is therefore expected and is not a failure.

Large-scale granular flows with open data do exist, such as the USGS debris-flow flume releases ([Iverson et al. 2010](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2009JF001514); [ScienceBase](https://sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/5b44ee03e4b060350a1408f1)). They are water-saturated and pore-pressure driven, so they suit a dry or cohesive Drucker-Prager model poorly. Field-scale MPM slope cases such as the Selborne cutting ([Alonso, Yerro, Pinyol 2015](https://upcommons.upc.edu/bitstream/2117/81776/1/2015_Alonso_Yerro_Pinyol%20-%20Recent%20developments%20of%20the%20MPM%20for%20the%20simulation%20of%20landslides.pdf)) also need pore pressure or strain softening. For cut and slope failure, the scale-free Griffiths & Lane slope is the practical choice. Its parameters are H = 10 m, a 2:1 slope, φ 20° and c 10 kPa, with a factor of safety of about 1.35 to 1.4 by strength reduction ([arXiv 2609.26104](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26104)). Scaled to 2 to 5 m with c/(γH) held constant, it gives a single pass/fail number for the cohesive presets.

## Tool-force data is sparse, and one open table carries the cohesive presets

For blade and bucket forces, the open data reduces to one table. Makange et al. 2021 ran a rectangular-blade subsoiler with **260 mm effective width and 16° rake** in a clay soil bin. The soil had bulk density 1667.7 kg/m³ and 10.8 to 11.2% moisture, with cone index 325 to 3031 kPa. The paper publishes mean horizontal and vertical forces at **0.15, 0.20 and 0.30 m depth and 1, 2 and 2.5 km/h** under CC BY 4.0 ([PMC8155074](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8155074/)). At 1 km/h, horizontal force rises from **1235 N at 0.15 m to 1905 N at 0.20 m and 3382 N at 0.30 m**. Vertical force is consistently about 0.40 of horizontal (490, 741 and 1390 N). Raising speed from 1 to 2.5 km/h changes forces by only about 1 to 6%. That makes the test quasi-static, which suits an implicit solver. The weakness is the characterisation. The paper reports DEM inputs (c = 10 kPa, soil-soil friction 0.45, soil-steel friction 0.55) rather than measured friction angle or adhesion. One depth must therefore be used for calibration and the other two held out for prediction. That DEM cohesion is close to the loam preset's 12 kPa, not the clay preset's 45 kPa.

The rest of the blade literature supplies methods rather than data. The D3K2 bulldozer series is blade-shaped and well calibrated. A 2025 DEM calibration reached **4.5% horizontal-force error at 1/8 scale** ([Powder Technology 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0032591025004516)). The series is paywalled and its cuts are sub-cell. Coetzee 2014 compared DEM, nonpolar MPM and Cosserat MPM against flat-blade tests in corn grains. Nonpolar MPM **underestimated draft at the shallower depth**, and dilatancy angle moved the draft force ([Springer CPM](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40571-014-0014-7.pdf)). Newton's 8 cm grid will make this bias worse for cuts of two cells or fewer, so the dry-sand preset should be force-validated mainly with cuts of 0.2 to 0.5 m. DEM tillage work points the same way. Ucgul's sweep models reached R² of 0.95 to 0.99 on draft with 10 mm particles ([Altair abstract](https://community.altair.com/discussion/40774/3d-dem-tillage-simulation-part-2-validation-of-a-hysteretic-spring-plastic-contact-model-for-a-sweep-tooloperating-in-a-cohesionless-soil)). Oversized DEM particles over-predicted ridge height in a cohesive Vertosol ([SCU portal](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/3D-DEM-tillage-simulation-Validation-of/991013001597702368)). The lesson for 4 cm particles is to compare forces before surface profiles. For the dry-sand and moist-sand presets, no open wide-blade dataset was confirmed. Ucgul's moisture- and compaction-varied tests ([Adelaide thesis](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/e53f026b-fb55-4682-99cf-e4c0dbc7f1fa/full)) and a newer open field sweep dataset in clay loam ([PMC13584141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13584141)) are the best leads. The sweep dataset's width and licence remain unconfirmed.

Wagner's fundamental equation of earthmoving for a flat blade in sloped Mohr-Coulomb soil ([arXiv 2505.19330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19330)) should be the analytical backbone of the blade tests. It lets the team sweep φ, c, soil-tool friction and depth for all four presets at full excavator-bucket scale. Vortex uses the same derivation, which also makes it a common reference point with a commercial competitor. Bekker-Wong curves play the same role for plate sinkage. Wong's rows give n = 0.7, c = 1.7 kPa, φ = 29°, k_c 5.3, k_φ 1515 for sandy loam and n = 0.5, c = 4.14 kPa, φ = 13° for clay ([Birzeit repository](https://fada.birzeit.edu/bitstream/20.500.11889/7935/1/An%20equivalent%20soil%20mechanics%20formulation%20for%20rigid%20wheels%20in%20deformable%20terrain%2c%20with%20application%20to%20planetary%20exploration%20rovers.pdf); confirmed independently in [arXiv 2603.28965](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.28965)). They are usable with plates 0.5 to 1 m across. Published dry-sand parameters vary widely between papers ([arXiv 2606.06790](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.06790)), so treat sand sinkage as a trend check only.

## Full-scale digging data exists only for a wheel loader with a published error budget

No public raw dataset of a full-size excavator digging in characterised soil was found, and nothing specific to the ZX200 was found. The closest Chinese studies instrument all six boom, arm and bucket cylinder chambers. A 36.5 t backhoe saw resistance **below 80 kN during initial cutting and above 200 kN near maximum depth**, but the soil description has no numbers and the data is available only on request ([PMC9519879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519879/)). A pair of 20 t machines, the closest class match, measured soil friction by triaxial test and reported a resistance model within 12% of tests. Their bucket size and data availability could not be verified ([Mechanics & Industry 2024](https://www.mechanics-industry.org/articles/meca/full_html/2024/01/mi230039/mi230039.html)). The useful open excavator data is kinematic only. The fuxi-robot dataset holds joint angles, images and elevation maps for real dig-to-truck cycles under a non-commercial CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 licence ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/fuxi-robot/excavator-motion)). It can validate operator trajectories and cycle times, not soil forces.

The one full-scale package with soil data, multi-channel forces and a sim-to-real error budget is Aoshima & Servin's study of a **15.2 t Komatsu WA320-7 with a 3.0 m³, 2.685 m wide bucket**. The material was crushed gravel of 30 to 40 mm, bulk density 1727 kg/m³ and repose angle 32°. Lift, tilt and traction forces and kinematics were logged at 100 Hz, and the pile was laser-scanned before each dig. AGX's real-time multiscale model reached **about 10% overall error, 11% on lift and tilt force, 8 to 19% on traction, +12% on payload and 21% on work**. Fidelity mattered little ([arXiv 2310.05765](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05765v3)). The 200 mm terrain grid ran in real time. Newton's 80 mm grid is finer than AGX's real-time setting, so 10 to 20% on bulk digging force is the right competitive bar. The caveats are real. There are only three loading runs. The supplementary page (umit.cs.umu.se/wl-sim-to-real/) is cited, but its contents and licence were not verified, and one reading found only videos public. The machine is also a wheel loader, not a backhoe. In the lab, Coetzee's corn-grain bucket tests found nonpolar MPM accurate on drag force to 600 mm displacement and **12% low at 800 mm**, while DEM ran 20% low at end of fill ([J. Terramech. 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489806000565); [J. Terramech. 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022489809000809)). Those tests set the MPM methodology but are too small for this grid.

The peer simulators show what counts as accepted practice. Chrono CRM (SPH, μ(I) rheology, BSD-3) reports **3.5% and 7.4% mean error on free-fall cones** in beads and Ottawa sand, a cratering-law slope of 0.1336 against an empirical 0.14, and a qualitative MGRU3 slip-slope match. Its RASSOR digging drum is checked only against Chrono's own DEM ([arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05643)). AGX's multiscale model agrees with coupled DEM within **10 to 25% on digging resistance** while running more than 1000× faster ([AMSES 2021](https://amses-journal.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40323-021-00196-3.pdf)). AGX also recommends calibrating penetration resistance separately against measured breakout force ([Algoryx docs](https://www.algoryx.se/documentation/complete/agx/tags/latest/doc/UserManual/source/agxTerrain.html)). Vortex's public material is the analytical equation above, with no experiments ([arXiv 2505.19330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19330)). Newton's `SolverImplicitMPM` is documented as experimental, with only a granular-drop demo ([Newton API docs](https://newton-physics.github.io/newton/api/_generated/newton.solvers.SolverImplicitMPM.html)). This leaves a clear gap: a ZX200 simulator validated against even three physical cases would exceed what Newton, and in part Vortex, can show today. Chrono CRM is open, so the same ZX200 bucket geometry can also be run there as a code-to-code reference, mirroring how Algoryx validates against DEM.

The order follows from both risk and dependency. The column collapse case comes first because it is cheap, needs no tool coupling, and isolates the two failure modes most likely to sink an implicit MPM at two particles per cell: volumetric locking and excess numerical dissipation. If the runout misses by 20% here, no tool-force result downstream can be trusted. The Makange table comes second because it is the only openly licensed cohesive force dataset and it tests rigid-MPM coupling, which Newton itself labels experimental. Its λ-sweep also yields the convergence evidence the team needs to state where the 8 cm grid stops being trustworthy. The wheel-loader study comes third because it is the only full-scale bucket package with a competitor's published error, which makes it the strongest external claim. It depends on obtaining data that may not be public, so the request to Umeå should go out on day one. If the data is unavailable, the fallback for case 3 is a code-to-code ZX200 bucket comparison against Chrono CRM, plus the 36.5 t backhoe resistance envelopes scaled to 20 t.

## Conclusion

The most valuable finding is the shape of the gap, not any single dataset. Every peer engine has avoided validating full-scale excavator digging against physical measurements in characterised soil, because no such open data exists. Chrono validates small, AGX validates against DEM plus one wheel loader, and Vortex publishes theory. A simulator that clears the three cases above can credibly claim parity with the field. One that also logs an instrumented ZX200 can claim more. That instrumentation means six cylinder-chamber pressures, joint encoders, pre- and post-dig terrain scans, and soil tests for density, φ, c and water content. Publishing that dataset openly would itself be a research contribution and a moat.

Two technical implications follow for the roadmap. Resolution, not constitutive physics, is the dominant validation risk at 8 cm. The harness should therefore report every result as a function of λ or cells-per-feature, and should state openly that cuts under about 0.16 m are outside the validated envelope rather than tuning parameters to hide the bias. The clay preset (cu 45 kPa) and the moist-sand preset currently have no matching experimental anchor, so they rest on analytical checks until new data is acquired: slope factor of safety, vertical-cut critical height and Bekker-Wong curves. Finding or generating data for those two presets is the next research task after the first three cases.
