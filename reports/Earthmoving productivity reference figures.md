# Handbooks bracket the simulator; bulking does not

Published references put a 20 t class excavator loading trucks at a 103° swing at roughly **14–21 s per cycle**. Bucket fill factors on heaped capacity run **1.00–1.10 for loam, 0.95–1.10 for sand and 0.80–0.90 for hard clay**. Hourly output for a 0.8 m³ bucket comes out at **about 125–175 loose m³/h on a 50-minute hour**. Against those references, the simulator's **16.6 s cycle**, **1.05 effective fill factor in loam** and **19–20 % excavator waiting** are all inside the ranges. Three of its outputs are not. **Payload per pass (1.55 t) is 15–45 % heavier than real loam would allow**, because the simulator puts a bank-like density (1850 kg/m³) into a loose bucket volume without bulking. **Single-carrier delivered output (149–175 t/h, about 81–95 m³/h) is well below handbook capacity**, and the fleet, not the dig physics, explains the gap. **Dozer force peaks of 90–175 kN exceed both the modelled 56 kN traction limit and the 122 kN stall pull published for an older Cat D3K.** Most handbook figures come from the Caterpillar Performance Handbook Ed. 29 (1998) and the Komatsu Specifications & Application Handbook Ed. 27 (2006), read directly. Hitachi publishes no estimating tables for the ZX200. No manufacturer publishes dump or haul cycle times for crawler carriers. No handbook curve values were recovered for a 9–10 t dozer, so several dozer and carrier benchmarks below are calculated from published factors rather than published as figures.

## 20 t excavators cycle in 14–19 s at 90–180° swing, before job conditions

The two manufacturer handbooks agree closely. Komatsu's Table 11 gives the **PC200/PC210 standard cycle as 13–16 s at 45–90° swing and 16–19 s at 90–180° swing**. It defines the cycle as excavating plus swing loaded plus dump plus swing empty, and multiplies it by a conversion factor for dumping condition and digging depth ([Komatsu Handbook Ed. 27, pp. 15A-9/10](https://pdfcoffee.com/handbook-komatsu-edition27-pdf-free.html)). That factor runs from **0.7** (easy dump onto a spoil pile, depth under 40 % of maximum) to **1.8** (small target at maximum reach, depth over 75 %). "Normal" dumping to a large target at under 40 % depth is **0.9**, and "rather difficult" dumping to a small target at the same depth is **1.1**. The source text is flattened, so the row/column assignment of this matrix is the researcher's inference, although the matrix is consistent under either reading. Komatsu adds a mining rule of thumb, **"every 10 degrees more swing will increase the cycle time by 1 second"**, which also appears in its Loading Tool Selection Guide ([Komatsu LTSG](https://www.komatsu.com/content/dam/komatsu/sales-and-marketing-documents/brochures/mining-parts/loadingtoolselectionguide-brochure-english-en-ltsgbr01-0522-v3.pdf)).

Caterpillar's Ed. 29 gives a printed "typical" breakdown for the **320B with an 800 L bucket in hard clay at 2.3 m depth: load 5.4 s, swing loaded 3.6 s, dump 1.8 s, swing empty 3.0 s, total 13.8 s**. The conditions are a 60–90° swing, above-average job conditions and an average operator ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, p. 4-148](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)). The same chart's shaded band for the 320B runs from about **15 s (fastest practical) to about 26 s (severe)**. Those two values were read off a graphic, not printed numbers. Cat's condition key places "loading trucks with truck spotted close to excavator" with up to 90° swing in its *Average* class, and swings over 120° in *Severe*. The handbook also notes that with the truck on the excavation floor, **"10 to 17 second cycles are practical"**.

The textbook route reaches the same band. Lecture slides reproduce what are almost certainly Nunnally's tables (the slides do not name the book, so they are secondary). For excavators of 0.76 m³ or less, they list **250 cycles/h (14.4 s) in soft sand/gravel/loam, 200 (18.0 s) in common earth or soft clay and 160 (22.5 s) in tough clay**, at 90° swing and 70 % depth. A swing-depth factor adjusts these: **1.15 at 30 % depth and 90°, 1.08 at 30 % and 120°**, and 0.83 at 70 % depth and 180° ([KSU CE419 slides, Tables 3-3/3-4](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/3.CE419-Ch3.1.pdf)). A 0.8 m³ bucket sits in the gap between the "small" and "medium" (0.94 m³ and up) classes.

The German BML guideline offers a fourth, explicitly conservative reference. It sets t = t_initial/(f_swing × f_depth), with t_initial = −0.50V² + 4.19V + 13.13 s for high-excavability material and −0.07V² + 3.30V + 15.52 s for medium/low excavability. V is the CECE heaped volume, and V_SAE ≈ 1.10–1.20 × V_CECE. The swing factor is f_swing ≈ 1.754θ^−0.1258 and the depth factor is f_depth ≈ h^−0.1164. Under this model swing changes the cycle by only about ±10 % over 45–180° ([Molaei 2025, Sec. 5.2.3](https://d-nb.info/1374860573/34)). Trade guides claim much larger swing effects, with 180° cycles 40–50 % longer than 90° ([ForConstructionPros](https://www.forconstructionpros.com/equipment/earthmoving-compact/article/10212146/dig-into-excavator-productivity)). That conflict is unresolved.

Applied to the simulated job (103° swing, 2 m pit, which is assumed to be under 40 % of the ZX200's maximum digging depth), the references give the following cycle times. All of these are **calculated** from published factors; none is published for this exact case.

| Reference | Basis | Cycle at simulated conditions (calculated) |
|---|---|---|
| Komatsu PC200 Table 11 × Table 13 | 16–19 s × 0.9 (normal) to 1.1 (small target) | **14.4–20.9 s** |
| Cat 320B typical + Komatsu 1 s/10° rule | 13.8 s at ~75° mid-swing + 2.8 s | **≈16.6 s** |
| Cat 320B chart band | fastest practical to severe | ≈15–26 s (read from graphic) |
| Nunnally small class, S ≈ 1.12 (30 % depth, ~103°) | soft / average / hard | **12.9 / 16.1 / 20.1 s** |
| BML, V_CECE ≈ 0.7 m³, 103°, 2 m | high / medium excavability | **17.5 / 19.7 s** |

Field measurements run slower than the handbooks. An Indonesian thesis appendix timed **30 cycles of a 30 t Komatsu PC300-8 at 60–180° swing (mostly 90–130°)**: digging 7.35 s (range 4.8–9.2), swing loaded 4.96 s (3.6–6.3), dump 4.23 s (3.0–5.3) and swing empty about 4.28 s (3.1–5.8), for **about 20.8 s in total** ([ITNY Lampiran C](https://repository.itny.ac.id/id/eprint/4294/12/LAMPIRAN%20C.pdf)). The swing-empty mean and the total were computed by the researcher from the raw values. In Baidu's autonomous-excavator study, a **human expert loading a truck took 23.6 s per loop (scoop 5.8, swing-to-truck 6.2, dump 5.8, swing back 5.7 s) and the autonomous system 25.0 s**. The test platforms were 6.5 t and 49 t machines, and the table does not say which was used ([Zhang et al., arXiv 2011.04848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.04848)). IMU data on a **13.4 t Komatsu PC138US** show loading cycles on a 12–24 s axis at 60–120° swing. In that data the experienced operator was consistently faster, staying above 0.8 relative to BML while the novice sat at 0.6–0.8 ([Molaei 2025](https://d-nb.info/1374860573/34)). **No open per-phase field time study for a 20–23 t machine (PC200, ZX200, Cat 320) loading trucks was found.** The figure of 9–10 s for a 20 t machine "if you are doing things right" comes from a vendor representative in the trade press and most likely describes trenching to a pile.

## Loam fills 1.00–1.10 of heaped capacity, but loose density sets the tonnes

Fill factors are well documented and broadly consistent. Cat Ed. 29 lists **moist loam or sandy clay at 100–110 %, sand and gravel at 95–110 %, hard tough clay at 80–90 %**, and well- and poorly-blasted rock at 60–75 % and 40–50 % ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, p. 4-119](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)). The Nunnally-derived table is wider: **loam 0.80–1.10, sand and gravel 0.90–1.00, hard clay 0.65–0.95, wet clay 0.50–0.90** ([KSU CE419 slides, Table 3-2](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/3.CE419-Ch3.1.pdf)). Komatsu inverts the clay ranking. It rates "natural ground of clayey soil, clay, or soft soil" as *easy* with K = 1.1–1.2, sandy or dry soil at 1.0–1.1, and sandy soil with gravel at 0.8–0.9 ([Komatsu Handbook Ed. 27, Table 9](https://pdfcoffee.com/handbook-komatsu-edition27-pdf-free.html)). The two manufacturers therefore disagree on whether clay over- or under-fills a backhoe bucket, so clay should be carried as a range. The only measured human value found is the Baidu study's **86 % fill for a human operator against 105 % for the autonomous system** in clean material ([arXiv 2011.04848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.04848)). That suggests handbook factors overstate human payloads by 10–20 %. No source quantified fill versus soil type at 20 t scale.

The tonnes per pass depend on loose density, and that is where the reference data matter most for this simulator. Cat's weight table gives loose/bank densities of **1250/1540 kg/m³ for "earth, loam"**, 1510/1900 for dry packed earth, 1600/2020 for wet excavated earth, **1420/1600 for dry loose sand** and **1480/1840 for dry clay**. The load factors are 0.81, 0.80, 0.79, 0.89 and 0.81 respectively, which the researcher converted to swell of about 23–27 % for earth, 12 % for sand and 23 % for clay ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, Weight of Materials](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)). Komatsu's loose/bank ratios are **1.11 for sand, 1.25 for sandy clay and 1.43 for clay** ([Komatsu Handbook Ed. 27, Table 1](https://pdfcoffee.com/handbook-komatsu-edition27-pdf-free.html)). The clay figure disagrees sharply with Cat's implied 1.22–1.25. A real 0.8 m³ heaped bucket of loam at fill 1.0–1.1 therefore carries **about 1.0–1.4 t (calculated)**, or about 0.65–0.75 m³ of bank material.

## Handbook output is 125–175 Lm³/h, and the truck fleet decides how much survives

All three estimating methods reduce to Q = 3600 × q_heaped × K × E / Cm. Komatsu writes it as Q = q × 3600 × E/Cm in loose m³/h. Cat's version is (60/cycle min) × heaped capacity × fill factor × job efficiency, and the handbook notes that the 60-minute hour is "never achieved in reality". Nunnally's is C × S × V × B × E ([Komatsu Ed. 27](https://pdfcoffee.com/handbook-komatsu-edition27-pdf-free.html); [Cat PHB Ed. 29, p. 4-149](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf); [KSU CE419](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/3.CE419-Ch3.1.pdf)). The job-efficiency values differ. **Cat's 50-minute hour is 0.83 and its 45-minute hour 0.75. Komatsu rates excavator efficiency 0.83 good, 0.75 average, 0.67 rather poor and 0.58 poor.** Cat's production table gives **168 Lm³/h for a 0.7 Lm³ payload at 15.0 s and 147 Lm³/h at 17.1 s** on a 60-minute hour. Cat's worked example puts a 320 with a 1.0 m³ bucket at 15 s cycles (230 Lm³/50-min hour) "near its maximum capability" ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, pp. 4-149 to 4-151](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)). The textbook's own worked example, a 0.57 m³ bucket in sand and gravel, gives **113 LCM/h**.

For a 0.8 m³ bucket on a 50-minute hour, the calculated envelope is **about 170–210 Lm³/h for easy digging, 125–165 for average and 80–110 for hard clay**. For average soil that is roughly **100–140 Bm³/h or 190–270 t/h** before truck exchange losses. These are researcher calculations from the cited factors; no manufacturer publishes a ZX200 or PC200 truck-loading output.

Truck exchange is the main loss term. Cat advises **including "approximately 0.7 minutes for truck exchange time"** ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, p. 4-152](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)) and budgets **0.6–0.8 min for truck manoeuvre in the load area and 1.0–1.2 min for manoeuvre and dump at the dump point** ([Cat PHB Ed. 29, p. 9-7](http://courses.washington.edu/esrm468/468%20Class%20material/PHB29.pdf)). Cat's 2025 pass-match sheet sets a tighter ideal: the **exchange should take no longer than one loader cycle, 18–24 s for excavators loading articulated trucks** ([Cat Pass Match sheet](https://s7d2.scene7.com/is/content/Caterpillar/CM20250318-49a43-6e751)). A Caterpillar Field Guide copy rates 0.7 min as good and 0.9 min as acceptable, but that is snippet-level and secondary ([pdfcoffee](https://pdfcoffee.com/caterpillar-field-guide-pdf-free.html)). Cat's load-time table implies **load time = first-pass dump time + (n − 1) × loader cycle**, because the loader meets the truck with a full bucket. The same sheet gives a "target truck load time" of 2.5 min for wheel loaders into on-highway trucks.

Pass matching guidance assumes big trucks. Cat's ideal is **3–5 passes**. Its excavator-to-articulated-truck pairings run 4–8 passes, for example a Cat 336 into a 740 at 8 passes, and Cat calls about 10 passes wasteful ([Cat Pass Match sheet](https://s7d2.scene7.com/is/content/Caterpillar/CM20250318-49a43-6e751)). No pass-match table covers crawler carriers. For an 11 t carrier and a 0.8 m³ bucket, the calculated count is **8–9 passes** at fill 0.9 and 1.6–1.8 t/m³. That number is legitimate for small carriers and should not be treated as a mismatch.

Match factor (MF) was coined by Morgan & Peterson (1968). It is computed as MF = N_trucks × loading time / (N_loaders × truck cycle time): **MF < 1 leaves the loader idle and MF > 1 queues trucks** ([Burt & Caccetta 2018](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/66822?show=full); [OpenMines, arXiv 2404.00622](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00622)). In an Indonesian mine, MF rose from 0.49 to 0.987 with six trucks, and seven trucks gave MF 1.15 with queueing ([IIETA](https://iieta.org/download/file/fid/130620)). The best construction field number comes from a Korean NATM tunnel. **One excavator with 7 trucks was idle 43.97 % of the time at 100.88 m³/h; simulating 10 trucks cut idle to 21.12 % and raised output to 141.78 m³/h** ([Kim et al., ISARC 2018](https://www.iaarc.org/publications/fulltext/ISARC2018-Paper160.pdf)). In the 13.4 t IMU dataset, idling took about 20 % of a 3-hour session ([Molaei 2025, Table 4.1](https://d-nb.info/1374860573/34)). No source states a recommended MF band.

## Crawler carriers publish speeds and payloads, never cycle times

The IHI/KATO IC120-2 carries **11,000 kg at 14,100 kg operating weight with 224 kW** ([KATO Works AU](https://katoworks.com.au/product/ic120-2/)). A dealer lists the IC120 at **8.0/10.9 km/h travel and a 65° dump angle**. The same dealer's vessel capacity, given as "7.5/10.0 cu ft", is almost certainly a units error and should not be used ([KATO CES](https://katoces.com/product/ic120-crawler-carrier/)). The closest Komatsu peer, the CD110R-3, carries 11,000 kg in a **6.2 m³ heaped (3.7 m³ struck) body** at 10.0/8.4/4.6 km/h, with a 60° dump angle and a **4.7 rpm** slewing upper structure ([Komatsu CD110R-3 catalogue](https://kcsj.komatsu/-/media/kcsj/contact/download/pdf/construction_machine/CD110R-3.pdf)). The Morooka MST-2200VD is rated at 11,000 kg and 0–13.7 km/h ([Morooka Americas](https://morookaamericas.com/products/mst2200vd/)). **No manufacturer publishes body raise/lower times, haul cycles or hourly output for crawler carriers.** The nearest references are haul-truck values: Cat's 1.0–1.2 min manoeuvre-and-dump, and a mine time study's **52.5 s mean dump plus 26.6 s mean spot** ([IJG 2023](https://www.scirp.org/pdf/ijg_2023081610254581.pdf)). Both overstate a small carrier on a short haul. A prior of **0.3–0.6 min dump-and-reposition** and **50–70 % of rated top speed** on site tracks is an engineering assumption, not a published figure. Carry-back of sticky clay is documented only by vendors: one study found it in more than 60 % of articulated-truck cycles ([ForConstructionPros](https://www.forconstructionpros.com/equipment/fleet-maintenance/article/22751263/philippihagenbuch-inc-reducing-equipment-carryback-and-improving-hauling-efficiency)).

## Small-dozer benchmarks rest on rated blade capacity, estimating speeds and a traction ceiling

The **Komatsu D37PX-24 is 9,300 kg and 66 kW net, with a 2.13 m³ (SAE J1265) PAT blade**. Its HST ranges are **0–3.4/5.6/8.5 km/h forward and 0–4.1/6.5/8.5 km/h reverse** ([Komatsu US D37PX-24](https://www.komatsu.com/en-us/products/equipment/dozers/small-dozers/d37px-24)). The PXi version is listed at 9.38 t with a 3.2 m wide blade on a secondary spec site ([Lectura-specs](https://www.lectura-specs.com/en/model/construction-machinery/bulldozers-komatsu/d37pxi-24-11680423)). The current Cat D3 is close: about 9.4 t, 77.6 kW and 2.19 m³ ([Ring Power D3](https://heavyequipment.ringpower.com/products/detail/106100-d3)). SAE J1265 and ISO 9246 ratings assume a flat blade face and, in the standard's own words, are **"not for predicting capacities or productivities in actual field conditions"** ([SAE J1265](https://saemobilus.sae.org/standards/j1265_200305-capacity-rating-dozer-blades)). Komatsu therefore applies a blade factor: **1.1–0.9 easy, 0.9–0.7 average, 0.7–0.6 rather difficult, 0.6–0.4 difficult**. Its production formula is Q = q × 60/Cm × e × E, with Cm = D/F + D/R + Z. The handbook advises **forward 3–5 km/h and reverse 5–7 km/h**, and sets Z = 0.05 min for torque-converter drives. These come from an OCR copy of the older Ed. 19, so they are secondary ([pdfcoffee Komatsu Ed. 19](https://pdfcoffee.com/komatsu-edition-19-productivity-pdf-free.html)). Nunnally's worked example uses **4.0 km/h dozing, 8 km/h return and a 0.05 min fixed time**. It also gives a field blade-load formula, **0.375·H·W·L (LCM)**, for a measured pile ([KSU CE419 Ch. 4.2](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/4.CE419-Ch4.2.pdf)). Cat's secondary-reproduced correction factors include **0.75 for an average operator, 1.20 for a loose stockpile and 1.20 for slot dozing** ([Scribd ENCE 420 dozer deck](https://www.scribd.com/doc/234212998/Dozer)). **The Cat D3/D4 production-curve values were not recovered**, and no published spreading rate (m²/h) for a 9–10 t dozer was found.

Applying Komatsu's formula to the D37 (a = 0.9, E = 0.83, level ground) gives **about 180–236 LCM/h at 15 m and 95–126 LCM/h at 30 m**, all calculated. With an average operator, the envelope is **roughly 70–240 LCM/h**. At the 5–10 m pushes typical of spreading dumped piles, the same formula gives about **450 m³/h ideal** at the simulator's speeds (calculated). At those distances fixed and manoeuvre time dominate the cycle.

Force references are bounds, not measurements. The older **Cat D3K LGP is listed at 122 kN maximum drawbar pull**, about 1.4–1.5 times its weight, which makes it a powertrain stall figure, not usable traction ([Boom & Bucket listing](https://www.boomandbucket.com/for-sale/2011-caterpillar-d3klgp-bulldozers/a4905384)). The D3K/D4K/D5K drawbar chart tops out at 180 kN for the family ([manuals.plus](https://manuals.plus/m/4193a3749220933504bb23453bc5da4bb36698f04c7a99f517a558d49209c967)). No D37PXi drawbar figure is openly published. A power check (66 kW at 3 km/h and about 80 % efficiency) gives **about 63 kN** (calculated). The commonly cited track traction coefficients (about 0.9 firm earth, 0.6 loose earth, 0.3 dry sand) were recalled, not retrieved, and remain **unverified**. **No open-access measured blade-force data for a ~3 m blade exists**: the relevant SAE soil-bin and draft-sensor papers from 1968–1979 are paywalled ([SAE 680612](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/680612); [SAE 760650](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/draft-power-optimization-improved-bulldozing-production-760650)).

The cycle-time comparison is meaningful and favourable. The Cat 320B typical cycle plus Komatsu's 1 s per 10° rule lands at 16.6 s, exactly the simulator's mean. The phase split is the weak point. The simulator's dig is 50 % of the cycle, against Cat's 39 % and the 41–42 % reported in mining studies. Its swings and dump are faster than any measured human operator. The total is therefore right partly because errors cancel. Two causes need separating. Part of the gap is phase-boundary definition: the simulator counts "approach" in dig, while Cat counts repositioning in swing empty. Part may be swing kinematics that skip the boom-raise overlap and acceleration limits that dominate real swing times. As a check, a 74°/s kinematic swing (PC200 at 12.4 rpm) would cover 90° in only 1.2 s, so measured 3–6 s swings are mostly acceleration and coordination time. The simulator should re-bin its phases to Cat's four segments and run a swing sweep from 45° to 180°. That sweep can be checked against Komatsu's 3 s step between bands and against the competing BML (±10 %) and trade-press (+40–50 % at 180°) sensitivities.

The payload comparison is only meaningful once volumes are put on a common basis. Handbook fill factors apply to **loose** material in a heaped bucket. Their densities are loose densities (loam 1250–1600 kg/m³), and bank densities are 20–25 % higher. The simulator models no bulking and puts 1850 kg/m³, which is a bank or dense-loose value, into the bucket. As a result it carries about **0.84 m³ of bank-equivalent soil per pass, where a real bucket of loam would carry about 0.65–0.75 Bm³**, so it moves 15–30 % more in-situ volume per pass. That explains the heavy payload, the low end of the loam pass count, and why a 10 t load occupies only 5.4 m³ in the dozer step instead of a realistic 6.3–8 Lm³. It also means a 0.3 m "lift" in the simulator is effectively a compacted lift. The fix is to carry separate bank and loose densities per soil and apply swell on excavation. Plausible values from the sources are about 12 % for sand, 23–27 % for loam and 22–43 % for clay. Output should then be reported in Bm³/h, Lm³/h and t/h side by side.

The production comparison is meaningful only at equal job efficiency and fleet balance. Handbook figures carry E = 0.83 and an assumed truck exchange. The simulator's delivered rates are continuous 60-minute values with real fleet interaction. Its single-carrier deficit comes from fleet balance: a calculated MF of about 0.7 and an effective exchange of about 57 s, near Cat's "acceptable" 0.9 min. Its two-carrier result recovers to handbook capacity. This mirrors the tunnel study's finding that truck count moved output by 40 %. Dump time and carrier round-trip time cannot be validated against anything published. The simulator should document its carrier dump, spot and turn times as assumptions, ideally against a video time study of an IC120. In sand, 9–12 passes implies a fill factor of about 0.75–0.9, below Cat's 95–110 %. That is defensible for dry, free-flowing sand spilling off the heap, but it needs to be stated explicitly, and the simulator's sand density should be reported alongside it.

The dozer comparison is only partly meaningful. Speeds and spreading time sit comfortably in range. With one load every 153–241 s and 60–75 s of spreading work per load, the dozer is about 30–45 % utilised (calculated), consistent with one small dozer serving one excavator. Mean blade forces of 5–28 kN lie below the traction ceiling, as spreading thin piles should. The peaks of 90–175 kN cannot be sustained by a 9.4 t machine whose traction is capped at 56 kN. They could be inertial: decelerating 9.4 t from 1 m/s in 0.1 s takes about 94 kN, which is plausible for a blade striking a pile. Above about 120 kN, however, they are more likely contact-stiffness artefacts. The simulator should report forces filtered over 0.25–0.5 s, check the share of time spent above the traction limit, and make μ soil-dependent rather than a fixed 0.6, which is likely too high for dry sand.

## Conclusion

The references support a narrower conclusion than "the simulator matches the handbooks". The excavator's total cycle and the site's waiting fractions are already credible. The two errors that would mislead an industry user are both bookkeeping, not physics: missing bulking inflates tonnes per pass and deflates loose volumes, and the dig/swing split differs from how handbooks and time studies define phases. Fixing density and volume accounting will move payload, pass counts, dozer spreading volume and lift thickness into line at once. Re-binning the phases will reveal whether the swing dynamics are genuinely too fast.

The larger implication is that the open literature cannot validate this site end to end. There are no crawler-carrier cycle times, no ZX200 estimating tables, no recovered D3/D37 production curves, no small-dozer blade-force measurements and no per-phase field study of a 20 t excavator. The simulator's benchmark logs could themselves fill these gaps if they are paired with a modest field time study on a real ZX200 and IC120. A video-timed shift of roughly 50 cycles per soil would be enough. It would also give a validation dataset that, on this evidence, no one has published.
