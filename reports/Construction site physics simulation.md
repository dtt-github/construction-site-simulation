# Run the soil on the Spark, render it in the browser

Yes, we can build a physically realistic construction site on NVIDIA's free SimReady OpenUSD assets, and we do not have to go through Omniverse or Isaac Sim to do it. The assets are plain OpenUSD files under CC BY 4.0. On this DGX Spark we installed `usd-core` 26.11 aarch64 wheels and read every rigid body, mass, joint and physics material out of a real asset, so a browser three.js pipeline is open to us today. A third party has already converted 2,595 of the assets to web-optimised GLB with the physics re-injected. The physics is the hard part, not the assets. No browser engine can do validated soil mechanics. The best open real-time soil method, a heightfield with a particle "active zone" around the bucket, matches high-resolution DEM within 10–25% while running more than 1,000× faster. Field tests of wheel-loader bucket filling show a sim-to-real gap of about 10%. The recommended design is therefore a hybrid. Newton/Warp (with Chrono as a second candidate) runs the soil, machines, hydraulics and cables on the GB10 and streams compact state to a three.js WebGPU client. A reduced, all-browser tier uses Jolt WASM plus a learned heightmap model for demos and many viewers. Omniverse/Isaac Sim stays optional: we use it for authoring checks, RTX sensor simulation and a few pixel-streamed "hero" viewers. Heavy machines, cranes, trucks, terrain and workers are missing from SimReady and must be built or sourced. Excavator-scale soil validation will be our own work, because no public benchmark exists.

Throughout, **[validated]** means a measured or peer-reviewed result, or our own hands-on test. **[vendor]** means a claim by the maker that has not been independently checked. **[inference]** means our engineering judgement from the evidence.

## SimReady assets run outside Omniverse, verified on this GB10

The feasibility review found **about 2,677 SimReady assets under CC BY 4.0**. They come with PhysX, Newton and MuJoCo payloads and cover barriers, scaffolds, ladders, beams, pallets, tools and forklifts well. They include no excavators, cranes, trucks, building materials, terrain or workers ([nvidia/simready-assets](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/simready-assets)). A hands-on inspection of `vehicle_forklift_orange_a01` shows what that means for a non-Omniverse pipeline **[validated]**. Each asset is a multi-file layer tree. A binary-crate root (Z-up, metres, kilograms) references `payloads/base.usda`. Three variant sets, `PhysX`, `Newton` and `MuJoCo`, switch engine-specific physics payloads, and PhysX is enabled by default. The physics is ordinary UsdPhysics. The forklift has 15 rigid bodies, an explicit mass of 158.8 kg on the body, 7 physics materials (for example steel: density 7800, static friction 0.5, dynamic friction 0.4), 8 revolute, 5 prismatic and 1 fixed joint, and drives on 6 joints. Collision differs per engine. The PhysX variant uses SDF mesh collision, which a browser engine cannot use. The MuJoCo variant uses convex hulls ([forklift asset files](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/simready-assets/resolve/main/vehicle_forklift_orange_a01/simready_usd/payloads/materials.usda)).

**The aarch64 question is settled by direct test.** One research note repeats Newton's PyPI statement that `usd-core` has no Linux aarch64 wheels, so Newton falls back to `usd-exchange` ([newton on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/newton/)). That statement is out of date. PyPI lists `usd-core` 26.11 with manylinux_2_28 aarch64 wheels for CPython 3.11–3.14 ([PyPI usd-core](https://pypi.org/project/usd-core/)). We installed it on this DGX Spark, and a `pxr.UsdPhysics` extraction script (RigidBodyAPI, MassAPI, the joint classes, MaterialAPI) ran successfully **[validated, hands-on]**. Trust the hands-on result. The offline asset pipeline (variant selection, flattening, a physics sidecar, USDZ packaging) runs natively on our hardware with no Kit, Nucleus or x86 machine.

Browser *loading* is less mature than offline extraction. three.js r187 (30 Oct 2026) ships a `USDLoader` that now parses both USDA and USDC and resolves references, payloads and variants. However, it resolves references only against files already inside a USDZ archive, it does not fetch sibling files over HTTP, and it ignores `physics:` attributes, sublayers, MDL and UDIM textures ([USDComposer.js r187](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/loaders/usd/USDComposer.js)). The forklift's textures use UDIM tiles 1001–1003, so they would not resolve. Full OpenUSD-in-WASM runtimes exist, but neither fits a product yet. `@needle-tools/usd` 1.1.2 (OpenUSD 26.05 plus MaterialX) is licensed **PolyForm-Noncommercial-1.0.0** on npm ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@needle-tools/usd)). `@openusd-wasm/*` is MIT but only at version 0.0.17 ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@openusd-wasm/core)). NVIDIA's own web route, ovrtx plus ovstream, renders RTX on the server and streams video over WebRTC. It does not render USD in the client ([NVIDIA-Omniverse GitHub](https://github.com/NVIDIA-Omniverse)).

The practical path is **offline conversion to GLB plus a physics sidecar**, and most of that work already exists. The `codepawl/simready-glb` dataset (CC BY 4.0, updated 1 Nov 2026) converts 2,595 assets from 228 GB to 3.2 GB using meshopt and WebP. Its median file is 0.42 MB. It re-reads rigid bodies, mass, joints and friction from the USD layers and writes them as `KHR_physics_rigid_bodies` plus node extras, including 17,507 convex hulls with a median hull error of 3.9 mm (12 mm at p95). In 43 test files simulated in Newton 1.7, every field it checked matched the USD ([codepawl/simready-glb](https://huggingface.co/datasets/codepawl/simready-glb)) **[third-party, partially validated]**. Three caveats apply. First, it covers 2,595 assets against roughly 2,677, so diff the asset lists. Second, `KHR_physics_rigid_bodies` is still an unmerged draft (glTF PR #2424), and three.js has no loader for it, so we parse the JSON ourselves ([PR #2424](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/pull/2424)). Third, the converter drops PhysX SDF approximations and may drop semantic labels. Materials need no MDL baking, because SimReady authors a UsdPreviewSurface network alongside OmniPBR, and its packed ORM texture already matches glTF's metallic-roughness channels **[validated on one asset]**. CC BY 4.0 permits commercial use, modification and serving from a web app, with three conditions: we credit NVIDIA, link the licence, state that the files were converted, and do not DRM-lock the GLBs ([LICENSE.txt](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/simready-assets/resolve/main/vehicle_forklift_orange_a01/.metadata/LICENSE.txt)).

## The browser can show the site but cannot dig it at full fidelity

The browser physics ecosystem in late 2026 is good enough for the machines and too weak for the soil. **Jolt Physics (JoltPhysics.js 1.1.1)** is the strongest all-round WASM engine for construction machinery. Its JS bindings expose a `TrackedVehicleController`, wheeled vehicles with engine and transmission, motorised hinge and slider constraints, pulley and rack-and-pinion constraints, height fields, Cosserat-rod soft bodies usable as cables, multithreaded SIMD builds and a cross-platform-deterministic mode ([JoltJS.idl](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics.js/blob/main/JoltJS.idl)). There is no tracked-vehicle demo, so that controller is untested from JS. The PhysX 5 WASM bindings (`physx-js-webidl`, community-built) add reduced-coordinate articulations, which suit excavator arms. Official MuJoCo WASM bindings (`@mujoco/mujoco` 3.17.0) are the most accurate for actuated arms but have no vehicle or soil layer ([physx-js-webidl](https://github.com/fabmax/physx-js-webidl); [@mujoco/mujoco](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@mujoco/mujoco@3.9.0/README.md)). No same-hardware benchmark compares these engines.

Browser soil is where the ceiling shows. WebGPU MLS-MPM demos reach **about 100,000 particles on integrated GPUs and about 300,000 on decent discrete GPUs in real time** ([WebGPU-Ocean](https://github.com/matsuoka-601/WebGPU-Ocean)). Those are fluids, though. We found no WebGPU Drucker-Prager sand demo with published numbers and no finished browser bucket-soil coupling. The closest building blocks are EA SEED's PB-MPM WebGPU reference implementation (BSD-3) and dimforge's `slosh`, a GPU MPM coupled to Rapier with kinematic blades in sand ([electronicarts/pbmpm](https://github.com/electronicarts/pbmpm); [slosh](https://github.com/dimforge/slosh)). Position-based MPM is stable at any timestep, but its stiffness depends on iteration count and its parameters are not physical, so it yields plausible visuals rather than accurate forces ([CRESSim-MPM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18437)). At 1–2 cm spacing, 300k particles fill only a few cubic metres **[inference]**, so even the browser has to treat MPM as a small zone around the bucket inside a heightfield site.

The platform adds hard limits. WebGPU's default storage binding is 128 MiB and its atomics are integer-only, which forces fixed-point P2G scatter. wasm32 tops out at 4 GiB, and Safari does not support Memory64 ([MDN GPUSupportedLimits](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/GPUSupportedLimits); [web-features](https://web-platform-dx.github.io/web-features-explorer/features/wasm-memory64)). On Linux, Chrome ships WebGPU by default only for Intel Gen12+ and for NVIDIA on Wayland. **Linux ARM is not listed**, so a browser running on the DGX Spark itself probably needs `--enable-unsafe-webgpu` ([gpuweb implementation status](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/Implementation-Status)). We found no complete browser excavator simulator with real soil physics, so an all-browser site would be novel. It is achievable at reduced fidelity: Jolt for a dozen machines, a deformable heightfield, a 100k–300k-particle visual soil zone with loose two-way coupling (force readback lags 1–2 frames), and Gaussian-splat surroundings via three.js r186+ or Spark ([radiancefields.com](https://radiancefields.com/three.js-merges-a-native-gaussian-splat-renderer-for-webgpu-in-r186)) **[inference]**.

## Hybrid heightfield-plus-particle soil is the only validated real-time option

Research on soil-tool interaction worldwide falls into seven method families. Each trades fidelity against speed differently, and only one has published accuracy at real-time speed.

| Method | Validated accuracy (best found) | Speed | Best open implementation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bekker–Wong / SCM (wheels, tracks) | Matches DEM drawbar pull "well" | 100–1000× faster than DEM; real time on CPU | Chrono SCM (BSD-3) |
| FEE / McKyes bucket force + heightfield | Quasi-static cutting only | Far faster than real time | Custom (ETH, UC Davis pattern) |
| Heightfield + active-zone particles (Servin) | 10–25% vs DEM; ~10% vs field | Real time, >1000× faster than DEM | None open (AGX commercial) |
| GPU MPM | Sparse; ETH full-scale RL transfer 2026 | ~10⁵–10⁶ particles near-interactive | Newton implicit MPM (experimental), GeoTaichi, Taichi |
| GPU SPH (Chrono::CRM) | 3 physical tests + DEM benchmark | "Near interactive" at scale; also reported 30× slower than real time | Chrono::CRM (BSD-3) |
| GPU DEM | ~10% vs field bucket filling | 10²–10⁴× slower than real time | Chrono DEM-Engine |
| FEM / CEL | 8.8% vs soil bin | Hours per case | Abaqus (commercial) |

The **Servin–Berglund multiscale model** keeps resting soil as a heightmap. Near the tool, soil turns into particles that can grow, shrink and merge, and an "aggregate body" carries their mass and momentum into the bucket's reaction force. Against high-resolution DEM, its digging resistance and soil displacement agree within **10–25% at more than three orders of magnitude higher speed** ([Servin et al., AMSES 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00459v1)) **[validated, sim-to-sim]**. Aoshima and Servin then compared DEM and reduced terrain models, ranging from 1/10,000× to 5× real time, with field tests of a wheel loader filling its bucket. The **sim-to-real gap was about 10% and depended only weakly on model fidelity**, and controller transfer lost about 5% performance ([arXiv 2310.05765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05765)) **[validated, field]**. This is the most useful single result for our design. Once soil parameters are calibrated, a coarse real-time model is about as good as a fine one for bucket-level outcomes such as loaded mass and work. Its limits matter too: it is one machine type (a wheel loader) in gravel, and the work was funded by Komatsu and Algoryx. The model ships commercially as AGX Terrain. AGX documents Linux Unity and Unreal plugins but no aarch64 build ([AGXUnity docs](https://us.download.algoryx.se/AGXUnity/documentation/current/getting_started.html)), and its soil-tool interaction block uses 2 CPU threads that do not scale with the number of excavators ([TERA, arXiv 2501.01430](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.01430v1)). The idea goes back to Holz et al. (VRIPHYS 2009), so an open re-implementation of the published method is legitimate.

The open GPU continuum alternatives are catching up but are less validated for buckets. **Chrono::CRM** (SPH with μ(I) or Modified Cam-Clay rheology, BSD-3) supports digging and grading coupled to multibody implements. Its authors claim 100 million SPH particles on a 10 km terrain at "near interactive rates" using active domains. It has been validated against three physical tests, including NASA's MGRU3 rover, and against a DEM RASSOR digging benchmark ([arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05643)). That evidence leans toward lunar regolith rather than wet construction soils. **NVIDIA Newton's `SolverImplicitMPM`**, exposed in Isaac Lab 3.x, handles sand and other granular media on an implicit voxel grid. Its rigid-MPM coupling is officially **experimental**, and it has no published throughput or excavation validation ([Isaac Lab implicit MPM](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/using_mpm.html)). The strongest sign that GPU MPM is good enough came in September 2026. ETH RSL trained RL in a GPU-parallel MPM simulation conditioned on soil shape and compactness, then deployed it on an **11.5 t hydraulic excavator**. The machine built a 42 m embankment in 45 minutes with 201 strokes and no failures, matching an expert operator's pace ([arXiv 2609.12677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12677)) **[preprint; task-level, not force-level validation]**.

The offline reference tier is GPU DEM. **Chrono DEM-Engine** scales linearly to 150 million elements on two A100s, and it ran an 11-million-element, 15-second rover co-simulation in under a day ([arXiv 2311.04648](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04648v2)). Fraunhofer validates excavator DEM by matching simulated triaxial tests to lab triaxial data ([Multibody Syst. Dyn. 2025](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11044-025-10061-5)). DEM is how we would calibrate the faster models and generate training data. It cannot run the live site. Coupled Eulerian-Lagrangian FEM gives the best single-tool cutting accuracy (8.8% against a soil bin, versus 13.8% for SPH), but at 6.6 hours per case ([PLOS ONE 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0322861.html)), so it belongs only in tooth and bucket design.

Three gaps limit how "realistic" anyone can honestly claim to be. We found **no excavation-force validation in wet clay or mud for any GPU method**, no community excavator-bucket benchmark comparable to the terramechanics single-wheel test, and no published quantitative error for FEE against full-scale bucket forces. Moisture and compaction are modelled in principle by AGX's per-cell compaction, by MPM Cam-Clay or viscoplastic laws, and by DEM liquid bridges and EEPA, but these models have not been validated for digging. Rocks need DEM clumps, rigid bodies in the hybrid scheme, or MPM-DDA coupling ([PARTICLES 2025](https://particles2025.cimne.com/event/contribution/f6b9199b-0d96-11f0-9835-000c29ddfc0c)).

## Machines, hydraulics and crane ropes need direct solvers, not game physics

A realistic machine feel depends mostly on pump and valve dynamics and boom inertia under load. Hydraulics is where open engines are thinnest. The real-time standard is the **lumped-volume model**: chamber pressures driven by bulk modulus, orifice-equation valves, and a load-sensing pump. The Parma group's coupled diesel-engine and load-sensing excavator model ran faster than real time and was validated against a real machine ([Casoli et al., CEP 2015](https://air.unipr.it/bitstream/11381/2789171/5/paper_CEP_revised_20150415_postprint.pdf)) **[validated]**. Chrono implements this model natively as `ChHydraulicActuator2/3`, with spool position and two chamber pressures as states ([Chrono API](https://api.projectchrono.org/classchrono_1_1_ch_hydraulic_actuator2.html)). Placement of the hydraulics matters. One Dymola-to-Simpack case took 46 min 42 s as a co-simulation FMU and 1 min 43 s as a Model-Exchange FMU ([Linköping ECP 148](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/148/024/ecp18148024.pdf)). We should therefore integrate stiff hydraulic states *inside* the physics step rather than co-simulating them at a coarse macro step **[inference]**. Most RL excavation work, including TERA on AGX, sidesteps this by commanding joint velocities directly ([arXiv 2501.01430](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.01430v1)). That is acceptable for autonomy research but wrong for operator-feel realism.

For tracks and tyres, **Chrono::Vehicle** is the most complete open framework. It offers single- and double-pin segmented tracks and ANCF band tracks on SCM terrain ([Chrono M113](https://api.projectchrono.org/group__vehicle__models__m113.html)). Chrono 7.0.3 explicitly fixed AArch64 builds ([releases](https://github.com/projectchrono/chrono/releases)), but its CUDA modules (CRM, DEM-Engine) have not been verified on GB10. Vortex Studio uses Bekker equations for track-soil interaction plus a DEM-mesh hybrid soil, and it produced dig cycles that transferred unchanged to ETH's HEAP walking excavator ([ETH Research Collection](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/entities/publication/85e9f204-6ca2-4073-ad80-ee4b891e7ee3)). It is commercial and shows no sign of an aarch64 build.

Cranes add the hardest numerical problem on the site. A light rope node sits between a heavy load and a stiff winch, so iterative solvers produce "rubber-band" stretch. AGX's guidance is to keep interacting bodies within about one order of magnitude of each other under iterative solvers. Its split direct/iterative solver is stable to about **1:100,000 mass ratio** ([AGX stable simulations](https://www.algoryx.se/documentation/complete/Momentum/source/Stable%20Simulations.html)) **[vendor]**. The proven rope model is Servin and Lacoursière's **adaptive-resolution lumped-element wire**, which removes nodes under high tension and is solved directly ([IEEE TVCG 2011 via DBLP](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/pid/53/4865.html); [AGX Wire manual](https://www.algoryx.se/documentation/complete/agx/html/doc/UserManual/source/agxwire__simulating_wires.html)). Open alternatives are Deul et al.'s direct-solve position-based stiff rods, about 100× faster than plain XPBD at 10,000 segments ([Eurographics 2018](https://diglib.eg.org/items/7e668ddc-ff48-4ad4-bdf6-167cc4a338ed/full)), and ALE-ANCF variable-length ropes in Chrono, which NTNU is pursuing for a real-time crane twin ([NTNU](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2998113)). PhysX capsule-chain ropes show instability with many segments, and as of Isaac Sim 6.0.1 Newton's Cosserat cable is not integrated into Isaac Sim ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/isaac-sim-6-0-1-newton-engine-cable-cosserat-rod-example-cable-twist-support-how-to-convert-physx-usd-to-newton/381163)). Payload dynamics follow from modelling the hook and load as full 6-DoF rigid bodies on the rope. Double-pendulum swing (Vaughan, Kim and Singhose, IEEE TCST 2010) and payload twist then emerge on their own, and quadratic wind drag plus a stochastic gust field covers wind. CFD-based tower-crane studies found peak wind loads shifted 30–60° from the expected direction ([Chen et al. 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/xml/10.1177/1461348419847306)). No peer-reviewed validation of any commercial crane training simulator was found.

For the rest of the site, scaffolds should be rigid members with breakable joints whose thresholds come from offline FEM buckling studies, where lateral buckling is the typical collapse mode ([PoliTo, Eng. Structures](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2690093/3/1704020_ENGSTRUCT.pdf)). Workers should be kinematic animated agents on navmeshes driven by a 4D-BIM schedule, following the Unity agent-based worker-equipment collision framework ([Sorbi et al., CONVR 2022](https://flore.unifi.it/retrieve/205992cc-4463-4404-b367-c8e604d86209/2022%20-%20Sorbi%20et%20al.%20-%20CONVR22%20-%20A%20Framework%20Proposal%20for%20Construction%20Site%20Agent-Based%20Simulation.pdf)). Musculoskeletal models are only for ergonomics studies.

## Learned surrogates carry server fidelity into the browser

Learned soil models are the bridge between a high-fidelity server and a lightweight client. Two families are usable. Particle surrogates are trained on MPM or DEM. Kumar and Vantassel's open GNS reports about 5% error against MPM ([arXiv 2211.10228](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.10228)). NeuralMPM's grid-based image-to-image networks train in 15 hours instead of 10 days and use 10–100× less memory than GNS ([arXiv 2408.15753](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15753v3)). The second family, **local heightmap world models**, is the better fit for a site. Umeå and Algoryx trained networks on more than 10,000 simulated loading actions in AGX. One network predicts the new pile heightmap and another predicts loaded mass, time and work from a 36×36 patch, and they were then used on a real Komatsu wheel loader by pasting predicted patches back into the global heightmap ([arXiv 2309.12016](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.12016v4); [arXiv 2501.06583](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06583)) **[validated in deployment]**. A September 2026 preprint uses a world model to rank candidate scoops. It cut scoop count by 17.1% and runs 5 candidates in 72.4 ms on a Jetson Orin ([arXiv 2609.15382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15382)). Fixed-shape patch CNNs fit onnxruntime-web's WebGPU backend, which supports GPU-resident tensors and graph capture but is still flagged experimental ([ORT WebGPU EP](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/execution-providers/WebGPU-ExecutionProvider.html)). No physics surrogate has been benchmarked in a browser yet. Sim-to-real limits are real: LSTM and TCN excavator models trained only on simulation struggled with real-world noise ([Chalmers 2026](https://odr.chalmers.se/bitstreams/82bf3148-bf47-4cc7-a941-b4f1d34eb996/download)). The fix is to interleave occasional physics or angle-of-repose correction steps, as the hybrid GNS/MPM scheme does, trading speedup (24× instead of 165×) for conservation ([arXiv 2309.13348](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.13348v1)). A differentiable GNS also recovered soil friction angle from video within 1.4° ([UT Austin ICES report](https://www.ices.utexas.edu/media/research/grand-challenge-awardees/final-reports/Grand_Challenge_Final_Reports_24_25_Kumar.pdf)), which suggests a route to calibrating against our own site footage.

Validation data is fragmented, but enough exists for a three-tier check. At the **force level**, Huh and Hong's IEEE DataPort set pairs real bucket-tip pressure, position and angle with point clouds captured before each dig ([IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/training-data-original-data-autonomous-excavation)). At the **deformation level**, there are Tohoku's single-wheel LiDAR rut scans in Toyoura sand ([MDPI Aerospace 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/12/1/71)) and before/after pairs we generate in DEM. At the **task level**, there are real excavator operation logs: the `hanfeng2006/excavator-motion` trajectories with elevation maps ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/hanfeng2006/excavator-motion)), the WIKA CAN-bus MCAP recordings, which may be undecodable because no DBC files were published ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/aiwika/Excavator-Embodied-Intelligence-Dataset)), and an announced but unverified 100-hour Volvo EC380 dataset ([HN](https://hn.svelte.dev/item/45362914)). Soil-parameter compilations, such as more than 2,300 cohesion and friction-angle records ([Scientific Data 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11655648/)), set domain-randomisation ranges. **No public dataset pairs excavation forces with lab-measured soil properties**, and OEM data from Komatsu, Caterpillar and Volvo CE is unpublished. A credible realism claim will need our own instrumented rig or partner machine data.

## The architecture: Newton on the Spark, three.js on the glass

The DGX Spark shapes the design in two directions. GB10 has 48 SMs, 128 GB of unified LPDDR5X and **273 GB/s specified bandwidth, about 205 GB/s measured** ([The Register](https://theregister.com/2025/10/13/nvidia_gb10_spark); [NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/detailed-compute-performance-metrics-for-dgx-spark/351993)). MPM and DEM are bandwidth-bound, so we expect roughly 1/6–1/8 of an RTX 5090's per-step throughput. We also expect to fit offline problems of hundreds of millions of particles that a 32 GB card cannot hold **[inference]**. A rough budget is about 1 million MPM particles at 100–400 substeps per second, which is real time only with a localised active zone **[inference, must be measured]**. Rendering, NVENC and physics share the same SMs and bandwidth, which favours **state streaming**: the client renders and the GPU is left for physics. Platform support is strongest for NVIDIA's own stack. Warp and Newton officially support Linux aarch64 with CUDA on Grace Blackwell ([Warp FAQ](https://nvidia.github.io/warp/latest/user_guide/faq.html)). Isaac Sim 6.x ships DGX Spark containers and a browser web viewer documented for DGX Spark ([Isaac Sim livestream clients](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/latest/installation/manual_livestream_clients.html)). That viewer is **one client per instance with no authentication**, and Isaac Lab's docs contradict it by saying livestreaming is not validated on Spark. Driver branch matters. NVIDIA staff say Isaac Sim 6.0.1 needs R580 (580.159.03) on Spark, and R590 crashes RTX initialisation ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/isaac-sim-6-0-1-gpu-crash-on-dgx-spark-gb10-arm64-driver-595-71-05/376418)). Our **580.178.04 is a later build on the same R580 branch, so it should be compatible [inference]**. Hold off any R590 upgrade until it is confirmed. AGX and Vortex document no aarch64 build, so licensing either one would probably mean adding an x86 physics node **[inference]**.

The recommended architecture has four layers. **(1) Asset pipeline (offline, on the Spark):** usd-core selects the Newton or MuJoCo variant, extracts a JSON physics sidecar keyed by prim path, and computes convex hulls where PhysX uses SDF. We start from codepawl's GLBs and spot-check them against the sidecar, and we model the missing machines (excavator, crawler crane, articulated dump truck, dozer) in USD with UsdPhysics joints so that one source feeds both server and browser. **(2) Server physics (Newton/Warp on GB10):** a site-scale heightfield in Warp. A Servin-style active zone around each tool, implemented with Newton implicit MPM, falls back to a custom Warp MPM if the experimental coupling disappoints, with FEE as a sanity bound. Machines are reduced-coordinate articulations with lumped-volume hydraulics integrated inside the step. Hoist ropes are adaptive-resolution wires solved directly. Workers are kinematic. Chrono (CRM soil, `ChHydraulicActuator`, Chrono::Vehicle tracks) is the second candidate, and the benchmark in Phase 2 decides between them. **(3) Transport:** rigid transforms quantised at about 2 KB per frame for 100 bodies (about 0.5 Mbit/s at 30 Hz), heightmap tile deltas, and only *moving* soil particles as 3×uint16. Raw 1M-particle float frames would need 2.9 Gbit/s at 30 Hz, so streaming every particle is ruled out **[computed]**. Viser and Rerun, both already Newton viewer backends, serve as debug clients ([Newton core concepts](https://docs.nvidia.com/learning/physical-ai/getting-started-with-newton/latest/newton-fundamentals/core-concepts.html)). **(4) Client (three.js WebGPURenderer):** meshopt GLBs, a compute-displaced heightfield clipmap, instanced soil particles, and splat backdrops from drone captures, which must be masked where we dig. An optional ovstream RTX pixel stream can serve a few photoreal viewers ([ovstream](https://github.com/NVIDIA-Omniverse/ovstream)). The **all-browser tier** uses the same client with Jolt WASM for machines, a learned heightmap-patch CNN distilled from the server, and a PB-MPM visual zone. It suits demos, training and many concurrent users, with clearly lower fidelity.

## Conclusion

The question has shifted from "Omniverse or browser?" to "where does each degree of realism run?" OpenUSD and CC BY 4.0 make the assets portable, and our own test removes the aarch64 blocker. Realism is limited by soil physics and machine hydraulics, and no browser engine handles either. The strongest evidence points somewhere less obvious. A calibrated coarse model performs within about 10% of reality and is barely worse than a fine one. So "maximum realism" on a single GB10 comes from calibration effort more than from particle count. The bottleneck is the data. The project's real differentiator will be the validation layer that nobody has published: soil-property-paired excavation forces, wet-clay behaviour, and crane rope tension.

Two risks deserve early spikes rather than late surprises. Newton's rigid-MPM coupling is experimental and is the load-bearing component of the recommended design. Chrono::CRM is the fallback, and the Phase 2 benchmark exists to make that call with data. Chrome WebGPU on Linux aarch64 is unofficial, so if the Spark doubles as a client workstation, a native or Electron shell may be needed. Isaac Sim earns its place for USD authoring checks against the PhysX payloads, RTX sensor simulation and a few streamed hero views. It is not on the critical path.
